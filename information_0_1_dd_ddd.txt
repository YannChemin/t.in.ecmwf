I'll start by reading the ECMWF page, then check supporting sources on the 0.1° product.

The main page confirms the 0.1° launch. The details that matter for your use cases are on the sub-pages, so I'm fetching those next.

Verdict: the 0.1° data is useful for Nepal/Bangladesh flood forecasting, but only if the ensemble is also available at 0.1°. For East African agriculture it is a modest gain for heat and a marginal one for rainfall. The ECMWF pages don't say whether 0.1° covers the ensemble, so that is the first thing to check.

What ECMWF published (live since 20 Oct 2026)

0.1° comes from the same ~9 km IFS forecast as the 0.25° data, and is published 2 hours later than the 0.25° data.
Surface and pressure-level fields are split into separate files, which helps users with low bandwidth.
Parameters are repacked at per-parameter bits-per-value. Precipitation is 12 bits, runoff 11, 2 m temperature and soil water 9, and radiation 10.
The main page lists the IFS streams as applying to both resolutions. The AIFS streams are published at 0.25° only, so there is no ML forecast at 0.1°.
The extra 0.1° parameters are described as mainly upper-air fields for Forecast in a Box and limited-area model boundary conditions, not new surface or hydrology fields.
Nepal/Bangladesh floods

Positives: at 0.1° the Himalayan valleys, the Siwaliks and the Meghalaya/Sylhet escarpment are better resolved. That matters for the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna headwater catchments. The precipitation, runoff, soil water and snowfall fields are all available, and the 12-bit rainfall packing is adequate.
Latency: the 2 hour delay is irrelevant for flood warnings that work in days.
Limits: orographic rainfall biases remain, and the extremes still need calibration against gauges. Open data keeps only a rolling archive of about 12 runs, so you must archive it yourself. There is also no 0.1° reforecast set for calibration.
Runoff: model runoff ro is uncalibrated and should drive a hydrological model rather than be used directly.
Bangladesh specifics: flood risk there is largely driven by upstream rainfall in other countries, so the 0.1° gain is mostly for transboundary basins. The Bay of Bengal cyclone season is active now. The cyclone tracks (BUFR) and wave fields remain available at both resolutions.
East Africa heat and water stress

Heat: 2 m temperature and dewpoint, 3-hourly maximum and minimum temperature, 10 m wind and radiation are all present. That is enough for WBGT-type proxies and for reference evapotranspiration (ET0) from Penman-Monteith. Finer terrain helps in the highlands, Rift Valley and coast, though it needs lapse-rate and station correction.
Water: soil water in four layers is available, but it is model-based and not calibrated to crop root zones.
Rainfall is the weak spot. East African rain is mostly convective and parametrised at ~9 km, so 0.1° adds resolution but little real skill.
Horizon: the open data stops at 15 days. Water stress and season-scale decisions, such as the current Oct–Dec short rains, still need subseasonal and seasonal products.
Access: the surface-only file plus byte-range reads of the GRIB index let you pull just the East Africa region. The cloud mirrors help too.
Things to verify before committing

Whether 0.1° includes the ensemble (enfo), and which steps it covers. The pages don't say.
Whether evaporation or potential evaporation fields are included. They don't appear in the bits-per-value table.
Real skill. The product is days old and there are no verification results yet, so this assessment rests on known IFS behaviour.
